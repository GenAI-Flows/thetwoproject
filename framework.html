The TWO methodology

Task. Workflow. Organization.

Redesign the work, not just the task.

TWO connects what AI can do with how work moves and who has the authority to change it. Use the three linked levels to allocate capabilities, design handoffs and review, and make accountability practical.

Start with one real outcome. This guide explains the argument, carries a claims-intake example through the levels, and gives you a process and checklist to try.

The missing middle

A faster task does not automatically mean a better system.

AI can draft, extract, classify, or summarize within a task. But a useful outcome also depends on the work before and after it: complete inputs, compatible outputs, timely decisions, and someone able to resolve exceptions. A faster draft may simply arrive sooner in the same review queue.

TWO calls attention to this missing middle: the workflow that connects activities to an outcome. Adding a tool asks, “Can AI do this step?” Redesigning work also asks, “What must the next person receive, what should be checked, and who can change the process?”

Review itself is work. Checking sources, correcting errors, recovering lost context, and integrating results can consume the time saved in production. We call this the validation burden, or “validation tax.” Its size must be measured in context; it is not a fixed property of AI.

The framework's proposition is that capability allocation, workflow design, and organizational rules should be designed together. It does not say that every task needs redesign, or that useful AI tools cannot improve an existing process.

Follow the whole outcome

In claims intake, extraction may become quicker while reviewers still reconstruct the evidence from scattered attachments. The design question is not only extraction speed: it is whether a complete, traceable claim packet reaches the right adjuster without avoidable rework.

Follow the example

Three linked levels

Keep the decisions distinct. Design their connections.

A task is a bounded activity within a workflow. A workflow connects activities and decisions to a defined outcome. The organization supplies the authority, resources, incentives, and operating rules around that work.

T · Task

Specify the activity and allocate capability.

Define boundaries, inputs, outputs, quality requirements, and the effort needed to verify the result. Choose what a person, an AI system, a deterministic tool, or an external provider should do.

Ask

  • What counts as a correct and usable output?
  • Is the input complete and permitted for this tool?
  • Can a reviewer detect errors, and at what cost?

Decide and produce

A task specification, a capability allocation, and validation criteria. Test capability on representative inputs rather than assume it from a demo.

Claims intake: AI proposes extracted fields with source locations. A rules-based tool checks required fields. A human verifies critical facts and resolves ambiguity; AI does not determine coverage.

W · Workflow

Design how work reaches the outcome.

Make sequencing, dependencies, intermediate artifacts, context transfer, and review explicit. Include missing information, corrections, exceptions, and escalation—not just the happy path.

Ask

  • What does each next step need to proceed?
  • Where does work wait or get done again?
  • When should review stop, return, or escalate work?

Decide and produce

A workflow map, artifact specifications, handoff criteria, and a review and exception protocol. Make the approved record distinguishable from an AI proposal.

Claims intake: route a traceable packet, not a free-text summary. Missing documents return to intake; conflicting evidence goes to an authorized adjuster.

O · Organization

Give the redesign authority and support.

Assign decision rights and accountability across teams. Fund review capacity and training; align incentives with useful outcomes. Establish coordination, access, retention, governance, and change-control rules.

Ask

  • Who can approve, pause, or change the workflow?
  • Do reviewers have the capacity and expertise to act?
  • Who resolves cross-team conflicts and monitors risk?

Decide and produce

An authority map, resource commitments, operating rules, escalation ownership, and measures of end-to-end performance. Naming an owner without decision rights is not enough.

Claims intake: a claims manager governs the pilot and routing rules. Reviewers have protected capacity; performance reflects accurate handoffs, not the number of packets generated.

Revisit the design: if reviewers cannot verify an extracted field, change the task output or keep that task human. If review queues grow, revise routing and capacity. If policy prohibits a data use, narrow the workflow before choosing the tool.

One worked example

From a claim submission to a reviewed intake packet.

Illustrative design, not an empirical case or compliance standard. This simplified property-claim example shows decisions to test. It reports no measured improvement. Actual insurers must set roles and controls for their policies, jurisdictions, and risk.

Boundary: start with an authorized claim submission and available policy record; finish when an adjuster accepts a reviewed intake packet. Investigation, coverage decisions, settlement, and payment remain outside this workflow.

Before: manual intake

An intake specialist reads forms and attachments, copies facts into the claim record, requests missing documents, and emails a summary to an adjuster. The adjuster reopens attachments to check facts and sends unclear cases back.

Possible bottlenecks: repeated entry, scattered evidence, waiting for missing information, and repeated review. Establish the actual baseline rather than presume which one dominates.

Task-only AI: a quicker summary

AI extracts fields and drafts the email, but the handoff stays unchanged. The adjuster must still find each fact in the original documents, distinguish facts from inference, and resolve missing or conflicting information.

Remaining or new burden: untraceable claims, extra corrections, and more packets entering a fixed-capacity review queue. A plausible risk, not a demonstrated outcome.

Redesign: a reviewable packet

Define the intake record and its acceptance criteria first. AI proposes fields with evidence locations; tools check completeness; people verify facts, handle ambiguity, and approve the handoff. The packet carries review status and unresolved issues.

What changes: the artifact, routing rules, review protocol, decision rights, and learning loop—not just the extraction tool.

The proposed sequence and its handoffs

  1. Receive and check permitted inputs

    The intake specialist confirms authorized use of the submitted form, attachments, and policy record under the organization's data rules. Missing documents create a request log; restricted or unsupported inputs stay on the manual route.

    Output: a document inventory and intake identifier, with missing items recorded.

  2. Prepare a proposed record

    AI extracts names, dates, reported loss details, and document references into specified fields. It marks unknowns and conflicts rather than filling gaps. A deterministic checker flags missing required fields and invalid formats. The original evidence remains available.

    Output: a draft packet containing field values, source file/page locations, missing items, conflicts, and tool/model version. No AI coverage or settlement decision.

  3. Verify and route

    The intake specialist compares critical identifiers, dates, and reported facts with sources, checks completeness, and records corrections. A routine packet proceeds only when required facts are verified, source locations are usable, and mandatory issues are resolved. Missing evidence returns to collection; conflicting policy or loss information goes to a designated adjuster rather than being guessed away.

    Output: a reviewed packet with reviewer identity, review status, correction log, and any explicit escalation reason.

  4. Accept or return the handoff

    The receiving adjuster checks that the packet meets intake acceptance criteria. They accept it for investigation, return it with a reason, or escalate an unresolved issue to the claims manager. Only authorized humans decide coverage and later settlement under separate procedures.

    Output: an accepted intake packet or a recorded return/escalation; receipt is not approval of the claim.

  5. Review the system, not only each packet

    The claims manager reviews errors, queue time, returns, and exceptions with intake and adjuster teams. The manager can pause the pilot or change routing and capacity within approved authority; changes to data use or controls require the designated governance approver.

    Output: a versioned change decision and an updated protocol. Review capacity, training, and incentives must support the new work.

What needs testing

Source-linked fields and explicit acceptance criteria may make handoffs easier to verify. They can also add preparation effort or create false confidence. Test whether the whole workflow improves at an agreed quality bar, including difficult cases. Do not remove safeguards solely because routine packets look good.

How to apply TWO

One bounded workflow. Six actionable outputs.

Start small enough to observe the work. These steps are a learning loop: evidence can require revisiting the boundary, allocation, or organizational rules.

  1. Choose and bound real work

    Name the trigger, intended outcome, recipient, and exclusions. Choose recurring work with accessible evidence and an authorized team.

    Output: a scope statement. For claims intake: submission to accepted packet, not end-to-end settlement.

  2. Map the current workflow and baseline

    Observe actual steps, inputs, artifacts, tools, decisions, handoffs, waiting, returns, and exceptions. Record who does the work and where informal fixes occur.

    Output: a current-state map and baseline measures, including adjuster review and returned packets.

  3. Assess capability and validation costs

    Test candidate tasks with representative permitted inputs. Compare AI, people, and deterministic tools against the same quality criteria; include review and correction effort.

    Output: task specifications and allocation decisions. Reject or narrow extraction tasks whose errors reviewers cannot reliably detect.

  4. Redesign sequencing and handoffs

    Specify intermediate artifacts, context transfer, acceptance tests, review responsibilities, stop conditions, and exception routes. Avoid adding steps that do not serve the outcome.

    Output: a proposed workflow and review protocol—for example, the source-linked intake packet and return rules.

  5. Define authority and operating rules

    Assign an accountable owner with actual decision rights. Agree capacity, training, incentives, cross-team coordination, data permissions, incident handling, and change approval.

    Output: an authority and governance map with resourced reviewers, not only an owner name.

  6. Pilot, evaluate, and revise

    Run a bounded, reversible pilot. Compare outcomes, log corrections and failures, and revisit earlier decisions. Decide whether to invest, scale, fix, or stop based on evidence and strategic relevance.

    Output: an evaluation record and a documented next decision. Expansion is earned by reliability, not activity volume.

Evaluate improvement

Measure the outcome, including the work of checking it.

A faster extraction step is not the same as a faster accepted intake packet. Define what “better” means before the pilot, including non-negotiable quality and risk criteria.

Quality and reliability

  • Output quality: accuracy, completeness, traceability, and suitability for the recipient.
  • Reliability: repeatable performance across routine, incomplete, ambiguous, and unusual inputs.
  • Exceptions: frequency, severity, detection, and successful resolution—not just average results.

Effort and flow

  • Review effort: time and expertise needed to verify a result.
  • Rework: corrections, returned handoffs, and duplicated work.
  • End-to-end time: from trigger to accepted outcome, including waiting.
  • Total cost: tools, integration, human work, training, oversight, and maintenance.

Accountability and alignment

  • Can each material decision be traced to evidence and an authorized decision-maker?
  • Do reviewers have enough capacity, and can the owner pause unsafe work?
  • Does the outcome meet the receiving team's needs and the organization's priorities?

A fair comparison

Use comparable inputs and case complexity, consistent quality criteria, and the same workflow boundary. Record sample size and limitations. Where feasible, use a concurrent comparison or randomized allocation; a simple before/after can be affected by learning, staffing, and changing case mix.

Document the model/tool version, settings, prompts, available context, human review protocol, and corrections. A changed model or protocol is a changed system that needs evaluation.

Starter checklist for your first pilot

An illustrative on-page checklist—not a certification, guaranteed benefit, or substitute for domain controls.

  • Write the trigger, outcome, boundary, and receiving team's acceptance criteria.
  • Map the existing sequence and include waiting, review, returns, and exceptions.
  • Specify task inputs and outputs, permitted data use, and human–AI–tool allocation.
  • Make evidence, review status, and unresolved issues visible at handoffs.
  • Name who approves, escalates, pauses, changes, and funds the workflow.
  • Set quality, reliability, effort, time, cost, and accountability measures.
  • Define a manual fallback, stop conditions, and a date to review the pilot.
  • Record what happened and why you will revise, expand, or stop.

Evidence and limitations

A research-informed methodology, not a universal performance claim.

TWO brings task capability, workflow coordination, and organizational design into one practical lens. Supporting research motivates these questions; it does not by itself validate the full TWO methodology.

Empirical finding

AI assistance can help, unevenly.

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's Generative AI at Work studies a conversational assistant in customer support and finds productivity benefits that vary across workers. It is evidence about a specific deployment, not a promised result for claims intake or a test of TWO.

Conceptual foundation

Allocation is about complements as well as substitution.

Autor's Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? explains how automation can substitute for some tasks while complementing human capabilities. This informs allocation questions; it predates current GenAI systems and does not prescribe this workflow design.

Read Autor's automation essay

TWO proposition

Design the three levels together.

Explicit artifacts, review rules, and authority may help local AI gains become usable outcomes. TWO proposes the workflow as a practical unit for these decisions, with continuous evaluation guiding resource allocation. This is a design proposition, not proof that every workflow benefits.

Hypothesis to test

More explicit handoffs may reduce verification effort.

Whether source-linked intermediate artifacts reduce validation cost, improve error localization, or help more in complex work remains a question for comparative evaluation. Greater structure can also impose overhead. The claims illustration demonstrates a candidate design, not a finding.

Where the framework stops

Results depend on data quality, model capability, case mix, domain risk, reviewer expertise, and organizational capacity. TWO does not replace professional judgment, legal or regulatory requirements, security review, or established process-improvement methods. An appropriate decision may be to keep work human, use a simpler tool, or defer AI entirely.

The framework foundation text is by Wei Chen and Hemant Bhargava. This guide draws on those TWO materials and their later workflow-layer discussion. It makes no claim of completed direct validation of the full methodology or measured results from the illustrative example.
